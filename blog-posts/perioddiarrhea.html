Why Do You Get Constipated Before Your Period and Have Loose Stools During It?

August 9, 2026 12 min read Menstrual Cycle, Gut Health, Hormones, Progesterone, Prostaglandins

Table of Contents

    Your gut isn't misbehaving—it's following your hormones. Understand why constipation before periods and loose stools during them are completely normal physiological responses.

    Have you ever noticed that about a week before your period, your digestive system suddenly feels different?

    You haven't changed your diet. You're drinking the same amount of water. Your routine hasn't changed. Yet your stomach feels heavier, your jeans fit tighter, you haven't emptied your bowels properly for two or three days, and by evening your abdomen feels like a balloon.

    Then your period arrives.

    Within a day, everything changes.

    Now you're making multiple trips to the toilet. Your stools are loose. Sometimes they're completely watery. You feel an urgent need to poop, and strangely enough, your menstrual cramps seem to get worse just before a bowel movement.

    If you've ever wondered why your gut behaves like two completely different organs within the span of a week, you're not alone.

    The good news is that your digestive system isn't confused.

    In fact, it's doing exactly what your hormones and inflammatory signalling molecules are telling it to do.

    Most people think of the menstrual cycle as something that only affects the uterus. Physiologically, that couldn't be further from the truth. The hormones and chemical messengers involved in menstruation influence almost every organ system in the body—including your gastrointestinal tract.

    Once you understand that connection, those monthly changes in constipation, bloating and loose stools suddenly begin to make perfect sense.

    The Story Begins Before Your Period Even Starts

    Let's rewind to about a week before your period.

    After ovulation, your body enters what is known as the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle. During this time, progesterone becomes the dominant hormone.

    Most people know progesterone for preparing the uterus for a possible pregnancy. But hormones rarely work in isolation or affect only one organ. Progesterone travels through the bloodstream, and wherever there are progesterone receptors, it has the potential to influence how tissues behave.

    One of those tissues is the smooth muscle lining your gastrointestinal tract.

    Unlike the muscles in your arms and legs that you consciously control, your intestine is made of smooth muscle. These muscles contract automatically in rhythmic waves—a process called peristalsis. These coordinated contractions gently push food from the stomach through the small intestine and eventually into the colon.

    Progesterone has a naturally relaxing effect on smooth muscle.

    As progesterone rises, those rhythmic contractions become slower and less frequent. Your digestive tract literally slows down.

    Food remains in the intestine for longer. Stool spends more time sitting inside the colon. The colon, whose primary role is to reclaim water and electrolytes before stool leaves the body, now has even more time to absorb water.

    The result?

    Your stool becomes drier, firmer and more difficult to pass.

    This is why so many women experience constipation during the days leading up to menstruation.

    Why Does Constipation Also Make You Feel Bloated?

    Constipation isn't just about not going to the toilet.

    Many women say they feel "full," "heavy," or "six months pregnant" before their period, even though they haven't eaten more than usual.

    That feeling has a physiological explanation.

    While stool remains in the colon, your gut microbiota continues doing what it has evolved to do—ferment carbohydrates and fibres that escaped digestion in the small intestine.

    This fermentation is completely normal. It produces beneficial compounds called short-chain fatty acids that nourish the cells lining the colon. However, fermentation also produces gases such as hydrogen, carbon dioxide and, in some individuals, methane.

    Normally these gases are produced gradually and passed without much discomfort.

    But when intestinal transit slows, fermentation continues for longer.

    More fermentation means more gas.

    More gas means more pressure inside the bowel.

    That is why constipation before your period often comes with abdominal bloating, visible distension, flatulence and a feeling of heaviness.

    For women living with conditions such as Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS), this slowing of intestinal transit can make symptoms even more pronounced.

    Then Your Period Starts... and Everything Changes

    Within the first day of menstruation, it almost feels as though someone has flipped a switch.

    The constipation disappears.

    Instead, you're suddenly rushing to the toilet.

    Why?

    Because your body has introduced an entirely different group of chemical messengers into the picture.

    These are called prostaglandins.

    Despite the name, prostaglandins are not hormones. They are hormone-like lipid compounds produced from arachidonic acid, an omega-6 fatty acid found in the membranes of your cells.

    Their job during menstruation is incredibly important.

    The uterus cannot shed its lining passively. It has to contract repeatedly to detach and expel the endometrium—the tissue that has been preparing all month for a possible pregnancy.

    Those contractions are driven largely by prostaglandins.

    Without them, menstruation simply wouldn't happen efficiently.

    Your Gut Doesn't Know the Difference Between the Uterus and the Intestine

    Here's where everything connects.

    Your uterus is made of smooth muscle.

    Your intestine is also made of smooth muscle.

    Prostaglandins don't exclusively target the uterus. Once released, they can also stimulate smooth muscle throughout the gastrointestinal tract.

    That means the same chemical messenger helping your uterus contract is also telling your intestines to contract more vigorously.

    Your bowel begins moving faster.

    Much faster.

    This increase in intestinal contractions is known as increased gastrointestinal motility.

    Food moves through the digestive tract more quickly than usual.

    Stool reaches the colon sooner.

    And, more importantly, it leaves the colon before enough water has been reabsorbed.

    Why Are Your Stools Loose?

    One of the colon's most important jobs is water conservation.

    Every day, a significant amount of fluid enters your large intestine from the small intestine. Under normal circumstances, the colon gradually absorbs most of this water back into the bloodstream.

    That is why healthy stools are soft yet well formed.

    But this process depends on time.

    When prostaglandins increase gut motility, stool moves through the colon so rapidly that there simply isn't enough time for adequate water reabsorption.

    The water remains trapped within the stool.

    The result is exactly what many women experience every month:

    • Loose stools
    • Watery diarrhea
    • Frequent bowel movements
    • Urgency
    • Cramping

    This phenomenon is commonly referred to as period diarrhea, and it is one of the most common gastrointestinal symptoms associated with menstruation.

    Why Do Your Cramps Feel Worse When You Need to Poop?

    If you've ever noticed that your menstrual cramps suddenly become intense just before a bowel movement, that's not a coincidence.

    The uterus and the bowel occupy the same pelvic space and are both responding to prostaglandins at the same time.

    As the uterus contracts to shed the endometrium, the intestines are also increasing their contractions.

    These simultaneous contractions can amplify discomfort, making it difficult to distinguish whether the pain is coming from the uterus, the bowel, or both.

    Many women even experience temporary relief after passing stool because some of the intestinal pressure has been released.

    Why IBS Often Gets Worse During Your Period

    If you have IBS, inflammatory bowel conditions, or a particularly sensitive gut, these hormonal changes tend to feel even more dramatic.

    Your nervous system, gut microbiome, intestinal motility and visceral pain sensitivity are already more reactive than average.

    The rapid shift from progesterone-driven constipation to prostaglandin-driven diarrhea can therefore exaggerate symptoms like bloating, urgency, abdominal pain and altered bowel habits.

    For many people with IBS, menstruation becomes one of the biggest triggers for symptom flare-ups.

    Can Food Make These Symptoms Worse?

    The hormones initiate the process.

    But your diet can absolutely determine how severe the symptoms become.

    During menstruation, your gut is already moving faster than usual.

    Adding foods that further stimulate bowel contractions or irritate the digestive tract can worsen diarrhea and cramping.

    Common culprits include excessive caffeine, alcohol, greasy fried foods, ultra-processed foods, very spicy meals, foods rich in refined sugars and artificial sweeteners such as sorbitol or xylitol, which can pull additional water into the intestine.

    Some women also notice that large amounts of dairy worsen symptoms—not because dairy is inherently inflammatory, but because lactose intolerance becomes more noticeable when intestinal transit is already accelerated.

    So, What Actually Helps?

    When your digestive system is already under physiological stress, this is not the time to challenge it with heavy, greasy or highly processed meals.

    Instead, think about supporting what your gut is trying to do.

    Hydration becomes particularly important because loose stools increase fluid and electrolyte losses.

    Potassium-rich foods such as bananas, coconut water, potatoes and lentils help replenish electrolytes.

    Magnesium-rich foods—including nuts, seeds, legumes and leafy greens—support normal muscle and nerve function and may help ease menstrual cramping.

    Omega-3 fatty acids from flaxseeds, walnuts, chia seeds and fatty fish may help regulate inflammatory pathways involved in prostaglandin production.

    Soluble fibre from oats, apples, psyllium and barley absorbs water and forms a gel-like consistency, helping regulate stool consistency without excessively stimulating the bowel.

    Warm, home-cooked meals are often better tolerated than cold, heavily processed foods.

    Simple meals like rice, curd rice, lightly cooked vegetables, buttermilk if tolerated, kanji, dal, soft khichdi and gently seasoned homemade meals place less stress on an already sensitive digestive system.

    One food that deserves special attention is ginger.

    Beyond helping nausea, ginger contains bioactive compounds such as gingerols and shogaols that have been shown to influence inflammatory pathways involved in prostaglandin synthesis. By moderating this pathway, ginger may help reduce menstrual pain while simultaneously calming excessive intestinal contractions.

    Even traditional preparations using fennel and ginger after meals may offer digestive comfort—not because they are miracle remedies, but because their physiological actions complement what the gut needs during this phase of the menstrual cycle.

    Your Gut Is Following a Biological Rhythm

    The menstrual cycle doesn't just prepare the uterus for pregnancy. It changes how your brain perceives pain, how your immune system behaves, how your metabolism functions, how your body retains fluid, and how your digestive tract moves food from one end to the other.

    The constipation before your period isn't random. It's the effect of progesterone slowing intestinal movement and allowing more water to be absorbed from stool.

    The loose stools during your period aren't random either. They are the consequence of prostaglandins increasing intestinal contractions, reducing the time available for water absorption and speeding stool through the colon.

    Once you understand this physiology, your symptoms stop feeling unpredictable.

    Instead of wondering, "Why is my gut acting up again?" you can recognise it for what it is—a normal, cyclical response to the hormonal choreography of the menstrual cycle.

    The goal isn't to stop these changes completely; they're a natural part of reproductive physiology. The goal is to support your body through them with food, hydration, movement, sleep and an understanding of what's happening beneath the surface.

    Work With Your Body, Not Against It

    Because when you understand the science, you stop fighting your body—and start working with it.
